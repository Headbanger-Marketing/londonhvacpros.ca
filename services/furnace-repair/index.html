Furnace Repair

What symptoms, recent repair history, and furnace details should I provide when requesting service?

Furnace service can involve correcting a specific fault, inspecting components, or deciding whether an aging unit should be replaced. Diagnosis may distinguish a flame-sensing problem from a blower motor fault because each points to different repair work. For maintenance, airflow, filters, burners, and the heat exchanger are considered in line with manufacturer instructions and inspection findings. If replacement is being considered, the home's heat loss and existing distribution help determine suitable equipment rather than simply matching the old furnace.

The assessment should record the furnace fuel and model, the symptoms and when they occur, and any recent repair history. Those details help distinguish ignition or flame-sensing faults from airflow and blower problems. For replacement, a load calculation and the existing duct arrangement inform sizing; age alone does not establish that replacement is necessary.

  • Flame-sensing and blower-motor fault diagnosis
  • Airflow, filter, burner, and heat-exchanger inspection
  • Load-based high-efficiency furnace replacement

Assessment and Scope

Questions for the Proposed Work

01

Separate ignition faults from airflow problems

A furnace that starts and then stops may have a different fault from one that runs but moves little air. Service diagnosis can examine the reported operating sequence and relevant components, such as flame sensing, burners, filters, airflow, and the blower. That distinction matters because cleaning or correcting an identified component is a different scope from replacing a failed motor. Do not open the furnace or work on its fuel or electrical components.

02

Use inspection findings to guide maintenance

Maintenance findings should be tied to the furnace model and manufacturer instructions, not an assumed schedule of parts replacement. Checks of airflow, filters, burners, and the heat exchanger can reveal restricted movement, contamination, wear, or damage that changes the recommended work. A documented inspection helps separate a needed adjustment or repair from a general tune-up, while heat-exchanger concerns require evaluation specific to the equipment.

03

Compare repair with a properly sized replacement

A repair decision depends on the diagnosed fault, the unit's condition, and its repair history; a single-stage furnace that runs for long periods is not by itself proof that replacement is required. If replacement is considered, a load calculation should account for the home's heating requirement rather than copying the old unit's capacity. Equipment type, duct compatibility, and manufacturer requirements help define the installation scope.

Compare the next step

Identify the work that happens before and after duct or electrical changes so a furnace fault is assessed in the correct construction context. Ask how equipment will be protected during dusty work and which operating checks follow completion. Keep required access open rather than plan the mechanical-room finish independently.
